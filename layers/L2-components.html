L2 · Component Laws

Constitutive relations for R, C, L in differential form.

Conventions on this page — All components are passive and linear. Constitutive laws are given in the récepteur convention (current enters the \(+\) terminal). Lowercase \(u(t)\), \(i(t)\) for time-varying quantities; uppercase \(U\), \(I\) for DC or amplitudes. Field quantities from L0 used without re-introduction.

Quantity Reference

§1 — Resistor

From field to circuit

Root: local Ohm's law from L0 §6: \(\vec{j} = \sigma\vec{E}\). For a uniform conductor of length \(\ell\), cross-section \(A\), carrying current \(I\) uniformly:

$$I = jA = \sigma E A \qquad U = E\ell \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad U = \frac{\ell}{\sigma A}\, I = \rho_r \frac{\ell}{A}\, I$$
Ohm's law — from local to circuit form

The resistance of the conductor is therefore a purely geometric quantity times a material property:

$$R = \rho_r \frac{\ell}{A} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \rho_r = \frac{1}{\sigma}$$
resistance from geometry and resistivity

Constitutive law

$$u(t) = R\, i(t)$$
resistor — constitutive law (instantaneous, memoryless)

The resistor is memoryless: \(u\) at time \(t\) depends only on \(i\) at time \(t\). No derivative, no integral — it is the simplest possible component law.

Power and impedance

$$P = UI = RI^2 = \frac{U^2}{R} \geq 0 \qquad \underline{Z}_R = R$$
resistor — power (always dissipated) and impedance (real, frequency-independent)

§2 — Capacitor

From field to circuit

Root: Maxwell-Gauss (L0 §2). A capacitor consists of two conducting plates separated by a dielectric of permittivity \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r\). A charge \(+Q\) on one plate and \(-Q\) on the other creates a uniform field \(\vec{E}\) between them. Applying Gauss to a surface enclosing one plate:

$$E = \frac{\sigma_\text{surf}}{\varepsilon} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon A} \qquad U = E\,d = \frac{Q\,d}{\varepsilon A}$$
field in a parallel-plate capacitor

So \(Q\) is proportional to \(U\), with the capacitance \(C\) as the proportionality constant:

$$Q = CU \qquad \text{with} \qquad C = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{d}$$
charge–voltage relation and capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor

Constitutive law

Differentiating \(Q = Cu\) with respect to time, and using \(i = dq/dt\) (L0 §6):

$$\boxed{i(t) = C\,\frac{du(t)}{dt}}$$
capacitor — constitutive law (differential form)

The capacitor responds to changes in voltage. If \(u\) is constant, \(i = 0\) — no current flows in steady state. It has memory: \(u\) at time \(t\) depends on the entire history of \(i\).

Impedance and frequency limits

$$\underline{Z}_C = \frac{1}{jC\omega}$$
capacitor impedance (derived formally in L3)
Limit \(\underline{Z}_C\) Behaviour Physical reason
DC, \(\omega \to 0\) \(\to \infty\) Open circuit — blocks DC Constant \(u\) → \(i = 0\)
HF, \(\omega \to \infty\) \(\to 0\) Short circuit — passes HF freely Rapid \(u\) changes → large \(i\)

Energy and power

$$E_C = \frac{1}{2}CU^2 \qquad p(t) = u(t)\,i(t) = Cu\,\frac{du}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}Cu^2\right)$$
capacitor — stored energy and instantaneous power

The capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates — it does not dissipate. Power can be positive (charging) or negative (discharging).

§3 — Inductor

From field to circuit

Root: Maxwell-Faraday + vector potential (L0 §4). A coil carrying current \(i\) creates a magnetic flux \(\Phi\) through its own turns. For a linear coil, \(\Phi\) is proportional to \(i\):

$$\Phi = L\,i \qquad \text{with, for a solenoid:} \qquad L = \mu_0\mu_r \frac{n^2}{V_\text{coil}} \cdot V_\text{coil} = \mu_0\mu_r n^2 V_\text{coil}$$
flux–current relation and inductance of a solenoid

Maxwell-Faraday says a time-varying flux induces an EMF. Applying it to the coil itself (self-induction):

$$u(t) = \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = L\,\frac{di(t)}{dt}$$
Faraday applied to self-induction

Constitutive law

$$\boxed{u(t) = L\,\frac{di(t)}{dt}}$$
inductor — constitutive law (differential form)

The inductor responds to changes in current. If \(i\) is constant, \(u = 0\) — no voltage across it in steady state. It opposes changes in current — it has inertia.

Impedance and frequency limits

$$\underline{Z}_L = jL\omega$$
inductor impedance (derived formally in L3)
Limit \(\underline{Z}_L\) Behaviour Physical reason
DC, \(\omega \to 0\) \(\to 0\) Short circuit — passes DC freely Constant \(i\) → \(u = 0\)
HF, \(\omega \to \infty\) \(\to \infty\) Open circuit — blocks HF Rapid \(i\) changes → large opposing \(u\)

Energy and power

$$E_L = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 \qquad p(t) = u(t)\,i(t) = Li\,\frac{di}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}Li^2\right)$$
inductor — stored energy and instantaneous power

The inductor stores energy in the magnetic field inside its coil — it does not dissipate either.

§4 — The C ↔ L Duality

Capacitor and inductor are exact mirror images — one swaps the roles of \(u\) and \(i\) relative to the other. This is not a coincidence: it reflects the \(\vec{E} \leftrightarrow \vec{B}\) analogy of L0. Knowing one completely determines the other by substitution.

Property Resistor R Capacitor C Inductor L
Constitutive law \(u = Ri\) \(i = C\,du/dt\) \(u = L\,di/dt\)
Responds to Instantaneous values Changes in \(u\) Changes in \(i\)
Impedance \(\underline{Z}\) \(R\) \(1/jC\omega\) \(jL\omega\)
DC (\(\omega\to 0\)) \(R\) — resistive Open circuit (\(Z\to\infty\)) Short circuit (\(Z\to 0\))
HF (\(\omega\to\infty\)) \(R\) — resistive Short circuit (\(Z\to 0\)) Open circuit (\(Z\to\infty\))
Energy stored None (dissipated as heat) \(\tfrac{1}{2}CU^2\) in \(\vec{E}\) field \(\tfrac{1}{2}LI^2\) in \(\vec{B}\) field
Field root \(\vec{j} = \sigma\vec{E}\) Maxwell-Gauss + \(Q = CU\) Maxwell-Faraday + \(\Phi = Li\)
Memory None Yes — \(u\) depends on history of \(i\) Yes — \(i\) depends on history of \(u\)
The DC limit rules are the first tool for filter analysis in L4. Before computing anything, replace each component with its DC/HF equivalent (open or short circuit) and read the circuit topology to determine whether the output is zero or \(u_e\). This immediately tells you the filter type.

§5 — RC Circuit: Charge and Discharge

The first ODE in the course. A series RC circuit driven by a step voltage \(u_e\). Apply KVL (L1 §4):

$$u_e = u_R + u_C = Ri + u_C = RC\,\frac{du_C}{dt} + u_C$$
KVL on RC series circuit

This is a first-order linear ODE in \(u_C(t)\). For a step input \(u_e = U_g\) at \(t=0\), with initial condition \(u_C(0) = 0\) (uncharged capacitor):

$$\boxed{u_C(t) = U_g\!\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)} \qquad i(t) = \frac{U_g}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau} \qquad \tau = RC$$
RC charge response — solution with \(\tau = RC\)

For the discharge (\(u_e = 0\), initial condition \(u_C(0) = U_g\)):

$$u_C(t) = U_g\,e^{-t/\tau} \qquad i(t) = -\frac{U_g}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
RC discharge response

Reading \(\tau\) from the oscilloscope

Two experimental methods (TP2):

Method How
Tangent at origin The slope of \(u_C\) at \(t=0\) is \(U_g/\tau\). Draw the tangent, read where it hits \(U_g\) — that time is \(\tau\).
63% rule At \(t = \tau\): \(u_C = U_g(1-e^{-1}) \approx 0.63\,U_g\). At \(t = 3\tau\): \(\approx 0.95\,U_g\). Steady state reached for \(t \geq 5\tau\).
In DC steady state (\(t \gg \tau\)): \(u_C \to U_g\) and \(i \to 0\). The capacitor acts as an open circuit — consistent with \(Z_C \to \infty\) at \(\omega = 0\). The inductor dual: RL circuit has \(\tau = L/R\); in steady state \(u_L \to 0\) and \(i \to U_g/R\) — the inductor acts as a short circuit.
