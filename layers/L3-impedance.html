Complex Impedance

\(\partial_t \to j\omega\). How sinusoidal regime collapses ODEs into algebra.

Conventions on this page — Lowercase \(u(t)\), \(i(t)\) for real time signals. Underline for complex amplitudes: \(\underline{U}\), \(\underline{I}\), \(\underline{Z}\), \(\underline{H}\). The real signal is always \(u(t) = \mathrm{Re}\{\underline{U}\,e^{j\omega t}\}\). Component laws from L2 and the voltage divider from L1 are used without re-derivation.

§1 — Why Sinusoids Are Special

A linear time-invariant circuit can contain any waveform. So why does the whole course focus on sinusoids?

Two reasons, which together make sinusoids the only input you ever need to study:

1 — Fourier decomposition. Any periodic signal can be written as a sum of sinusoids (Fourier series). Any aperiodic signal can be written as a continuous integral of sinusoids (Fourier transform). The square wave, the triangle wave, the arbitrary sensor output — all are sums of sinusoids of the form \(X_0\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\).

2 — Sinusoids are eigenfunctions of linear circuits. If you feed a sinusoid at frequency \(\omega\) into any linear circuit, the output is also a sinusoid at exactly the same \(\omega\). Only the amplitude and phase can change. This is not true of other waveforms — a square wave input produces an output that looks nothing like a square wave once filtered. So the response to one sinusoid completely characterises the circuit at that frequency.

These two facts together mean: study \(|\underline{H}(\omega)|\) and \(\arg(\underline{H}(\omega))\) as functions of \(\omega\), and you know everything about how the circuit treats any signal.

§2 — Complex Representation of Sinusoids

A real sinusoidal signal is written:

$$x(t) = X_0 \cos(\omega t + \varphi)$$
real sinusoidal signal — time domain

Using Euler's formula \(e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta\), we can write \(\cos\theta = \mathrm{Re}\{e^{j\theta}\}\). So:

$$x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{X_0\,e^{j(\omega t + \varphi)}\right\} = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{\underbrace{X_0 e^{j\varphi}}_{\displaystyle\underline{X}}\, e^{j\omega t}\right\}$$
real signal as real part of a rotating complex vector

The complex amplitude (phasor) \(\underline{X} = X_0 e^{j\varphi}\) is a fixed complex number that encodes everything about the signal except the time evolution \(e^{j\omega t}\), which is the same for every signal in the circuit at frequency \(\omega\).

The key move: \(d/dt \to j\omega\)

What happens when we differentiate \(x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\{\underline{X}\,e^{j\omega t}\}\)?

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{\underline{X}\,\frac{d}{dt}e^{j\omega t}\right\} = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{j\omega\,\underline{X}\,e^{j\omega t}\right\}$$
differentiation in sinusoidal regime

Differentiation in the time domain corresponds to multiplication by \(j\omega\) in the phasor domain. This is the central algebraic trick of the whole course:

$$\boxed{\frac{d}{dt} \;\longleftrightarrow\; j\omega}$$
the \(\partial_t \to j\omega\) substitution — turns ODEs into algebra

Connecting to the coordinate notation: the phasor \(\underline{X}\) is the signal expressed in the frequency-domain basis. The substitution \(\partial_t \to j\omega\) is the coordinate change from the time domain to the phasor domain. Just as \((1,\pi)|_\text{pol}\) and \((-1,0)|_\text{cart}\) represent the same geometric point, \(x(t) = X_0\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\) and \(\underline{X} = X_0 e^{j\varphi}\) represent the same signal — in different bases.

§3 — Complex Impedance

Apply \(\partial_t \to j\omega\) to each constitutive law from L2. In sinusoidal regime, \(u(t) \leftrightarrow \underline{U}\) and \(i(t) \leftrightarrow \underline{I}\):

ComponentTime-domain lawPhasor domainImpedance \(\underline{Z} = \underline{U}/\underline{I}\)
Resistor \(u = Ri\) \(\underline{U} = R\,\underline{I}\) \(\underline{Z}_R = R\)
Capacitor \(i = C\,du/dt\) \(\underline{I} = jC\omega\,\underline{U}\) \(\underline{Z}_C = \dfrac{1}{jC\omega}\)
Inductor \(u = L\,di/dt\) \(\underline{U} = jL\omega\,\underline{I}\) \(\underline{Z}_L = jL\omega\)

KVL and KCL hold unchanged for phasors — the derivations in L1 apply identically with \(\underline{U}_k\) and \(\underline{I}_k\). The voltage divider from L1 §5 becomes:

$$\underline{U}_s = \frac{\underline{Z}_2}{\underline{Z}_1 + \underline{Z}_2}\,\underline{U}_e$$
voltage divider — general complex impedance form

Module and argument of \(\underline{Z}\)

Writing \(\underline{Z} = |\underline{Z}|\,e^{j\psi}\):

  • \(|\underline{Z}|\) — ratio of voltage amplitude to current amplitude: \(U_0 = |\underline{Z}|\,I_0\)
  • \(\psi = \arg(\underline{Z})\) — phase of \(u\) relative to \(i\). For \(\underline{Z}_C\): \(\psi = -\pi/2\) (current leads voltage by 90°). For \(\underline{Z}_L\): \(\psi = +\pi/2\) (current lags voltage by 90°).
  • \(\mathrm{Re}(\underline{Z})\) — resistance: energy dissipated per cycle
  • \(\mathrm{Im}(\underline{Z})\) — reactance: energy stored and returned per cycle

§4 — Transfer Function \(\underline{H}(\omega)\)

For a two-port circuit with input \(\underline{U}_e\) and output \(\underline{U}_s\), the transfer function is:

$$\underline{H}(\omega) = \frac{\underline{U}_s}{\underline{U}_e}$$
transfer function — definition

It is a complex function of \(\omega\). Its two components carry all the information about how the circuit modifies a sinusoidal input:

$$G_v(\omega) = |\underline{H}(\omega)| \qquad \Delta\varphi(\omega) = \arg(\underline{H}(\omega))$$
voltage gain and phase shift from \(\underline{H}\)

\(G_v\) tells you how much the amplitude is scaled. \(\Delta\varphi\) tells you how much the output lags or leads the input. Both are measurable directly on the oscilloscope: \(G_v = U_s^\text{pp}/U_e^\text{pp}\), and \(\Delta\varphi = (2\pi\,\Delta t)/T\) where \(\Delta t\) is the time shift between the two waveforms.

Worked example — RC low-pass filter

Apply the voltage divider with \(\underline{Z}_C\) (output) and \(\underline{Z}_R\) (series):

$$\underline{H}(\omega) = \frac{\underline{Z}_C}{\underline{Z}_R + \underline{Z}_C} = \frac{1/jC\omega}{R + 1/jC\omega} = \frac{1}{1 + jRC\omega}$$
RC low-pass transfer function — from voltage divider

Setting \(\omega_0 = 1/RC\) and \(x = \omega/\omega_0\):

$$\underline{H}(\omega) = \frac{1}{1+jx} \qquad G_v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}} \qquad \Delta\varphi = -\arctan(x)$$
RC low-pass — gain and phase in normalised form

At \(\omega = \omega_0\) (i.e. \(x = 1\)): \(G_v = 1/\sqrt{2}\) and \(\Delta\varphi = -\pi/4\). This is the cutoff frequency — the \(-3\,\text{dB}\) point. The full Bode diagram treatment is in L4.

To measure \(\Delta\varphi\) on the oscilloscope: display both channels, read the time delay \(\Delta t\) between the two zero-crossings (or peaks), then \(\Delta\varphi = 2\pi\,\Delta t / T\). The built-in phase measurement function does this automatically. At the cutoff frequency, you should see exactly \(-45°\) for an RC low-pass.

§5 — Reading \(\underline{H}\) Geometrically

Since \(\underline{H}(\omega) = \underline{Z}_2/(\underline{Z}_1 + \underline{Z}_2)\) for a voltage divider, and all three quantities are complex numbers, you can read gain and phase shift directly from the complex plane without computing.

Write \(\underline{H} = A/B\) where \(A = \underline{Z}_2\) and \(B = \underline{Z}_1 + \underline{Z}_2\):

  • \(|\underline{H}| = |A|/|B|\) — ratio of the two moduli (lengths of the vectors)
  • \(\arg(\underline{H}) = \arg(A) - \arg(B)\) — difference of the two arguments (angles)

At low frequency (\(\omega \to 0\)): \(\underline{Z}_C \to \infty\), so \(B \approx A\), giving \(|\underline{H}| \to 1\) and \(\arg(\underline{H}) \to 0\). The circuit passes DC unchanged — as expected for a low-pass. At high frequency (\(\omega \to \infty\)): \(\underline{Z}_C \to 0\), so \(A \to 0\) and \(|\underline{H}| \to 0\). The capacitor becomes a short circuit and the output collapses.

This geometric reading is the fastest way to identify filter type and sketch the Bode diagram without calculating. It is the first step of every filter problem in L4.
