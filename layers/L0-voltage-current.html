Voltage & Current

What charge, potential, and current actually are — from the Coulomb field to \(U = V_A - V_B\).

Conventions on this page — Following the leçons: source point is \(P\), observation point is \(M\). Unit vector from \(P\) to \(M\) is \(\vec{u}_{PM} = \overrightarrow{PM}/\|\overrightarrow{PM}\|\). Volume element is \(d\tau\), surface element is \(d\vec{S}\), line element is \(d\vec{l}\,\). Differential operators are written \(\text{div}\), \(\text{rot}\), \(\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\) (not \(\nabla\cdot\), \(\nabla\times\), \(\nabla\)). Rank superscript: ⁽⁰⁾ scalar, ⁽¹⁾ vector, ⁽²⁾ rank-2 tensor. SI dimensions use M (mass), L (length), T (time), I (current); zero-power factors omitted.

Structural Analogy: Electrostatics ↔ Magnetostatics

The leçons (formulaire) make this explicit: every electrostatic concept has a magnetostatic mirror image. The analogy is exact in the static regime and breaks in dynamic regimes (where Maxwell's corrections appear). Knowing it halves the number of things to memorise.

ElectrostaticsMagnetostatics
Source: charge \(\rho\,d\tau\)Source: current \(\vec{j}\,d\tau\) (moving charge)
Field: \(\vec{E}\) — radial, polar vectorField: \(\vec{B}\) — orthoradial, axial pseudo-vector
Scalar potential \(V\): \(\vec{E} = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V\)Vector potential \(\vec{A}\): \(\vec{B} = \text{rot}\,\vec{A}\)
Constant \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\)Constant \(\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\)
Maxwell-Gauss: \(\text{div}\,\vec{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\)Maxwell flux: \(\text{div}\,\vec{B} = 0\)
Maxwell-Faraday (static): \(\text{rot}\,\vec{E} = \vec{0}\)Maxwell-Ampère (static): \(\text{rot}\,\vec{B} = \mu_0\vec{j}\)
Poisson: \(\Delta V + \rho/\varepsilon_0 = 0\)Poisson vecteur: \(\Delta\vec{A} - \mu_0\vec{j} = 0\) (Coulomb gauge)
The analogy breaks in dynamics. \(\vec{B} = \text{rot}\,\vec{A}\) holds always. But \(\vec{E} = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V\) only holds in statics — in the general case (CM5): \(\vec{E} = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V - \partial\vec{A}/\partial t\). See §5.

§1 — Electric Charge

Charge is the primitive of electromagnetism. It is a property of matter — an extensive quantity characterising the electromagnetic interactions a particle exerts and undergoes (CM2, §1.1). Two signs exist: positive and negative. The net charge of a closed system is conserved under any transformation, and is invariant under change of reference frame.

At the macroscopic scale, charge is treated as continuous and described by the volume charge density \(\rho(M, t)\). The total charge in a volume \(\mathcal{V}\) is:

$$Q = \iiint_{\mathcal{V}} \rho(M,t)\, d\tau$$
total charge in a volume — integral form

Conservation — continuity equation

Charge conservation has a local differential form — the continuity equation — linking the time variation of \(\rho\) at a point to the divergence of the current density \(\vec{j}\) leaving that point:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \text{div}\,\vec{j} = 0$$
continuity equation — local (differential) form

Reading it: if \(\rho\) decreases at a point (\(\partial\rho/\partial t < 0\)), charge is flowing away (\(\text{div}\,\vec{j} > 0\)). Integrating over a volume \(\mathcal{V}\) with boundary \(\partial\mathcal{V}\) and applying Green-Ostrogradski gives the integral form:

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = -\oiint_{\partial\mathcal{V}} \vec{j} \cdot d\vec{S}$$
continuity equation — integral form

This is the direct ancestor of Kirchhoff's current law. In the circuit limit — a node with several branches — it becomes \(\sum_k I_k = 0\). See L1.

The continuity equation is not an independent postulate: it follows from taking \(\text{div}\) of Maxwell-Ampère (\(\text{rot}\,\vec{B} = \mu_0\vec{j}\)) and using the identity \(\text{div}(\text{rot}) \equiv 0\). Charge conservation is thus a logical consequence of the Maxwell equations taken together.

§2 — The Electric Field

Following Maxwell's approach (CM2, §2.1): rather than action at a distance (Coulomb), a charge at \(P\) creates a field \(\vec{E}(M)\) at every point \(M\) in space, and a second charge at \(M\) interacts locally with this field. The force on a test charge \(q_M\) at \(M\) is:

$$\vec{F}_{P \to M} = q_M\, \vec{E}(M) \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \vec{E}(M) = \frac{\vec{F}_{P\to M}}{q_M}$$
electric field as force per unit test charge

For a point charge \(q\) at \(P\), placing the origin at \(P\) and writing \(r = PM\):

$$\vec{E}(M) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec{u}_{PM}}{r^2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,\vec{u}_r$$
Coulomb field of a point charge — Biot-Savart analogue for \(\vec{E}\)

For a continuous 3D distribution of charges, superposition gives:

$$\vec{E}(M) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \iiint_\tau \frac{\rho(P)}{PM^2}\,\vec{u}_{PM}\, d\tau$$
electric field of a volume distribution — integral form

Maxwell-Gauss equation

The local form of Gauss's theorem — the first Maxwell equation — states that the divergence of \(\vec{E}\) at any point equals the local charge density divided by \(\varepsilon_0\):

$$\text{div}\,\vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$
Maxwell-Gauss — local (differential) form

Integrating over a volume and applying Green-Ostrogradski (théorème de Gauss):

$$\oiint_{S} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{Q_\text{int}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
Maxwell-Gauss — integral form (théorème de Gauss)

Where \(Q_\text{int}\) is the total charge enclosed by the closed surface \(S\). If \(q_P > 0\), field lines diverge outward from \(P\); if \(q_P < 0\), they converge toward \(P\). Intensity decreases as \(1/r^2\); \(\vec{E}\) is undefined at \(r = 0\).

§3 — The Magnetic Field

A charge \(dq\) moving with velocity \(\vec{v}\) at \(P\) creates a magnetic field at \(M\) (postulate of Biot-Savart):

$$d\vec{B}(M) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{dq\,\vec{v} \wedge \vec{u}_{PM}}{PM^2}$$
Biot-Savart — elementary contribution

For a 3D current distribution with density \(\vec{j}\):

$$\vec{B}(M) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \iiint_\tau \frac{\vec{j}(P) \wedge \vec{u}_{PM}}{PM^2}\,d\tau$$
magnetic field of a volume distribution — integral form

\(\vec{B}\) is an orthoradial (axial) pseudo-vector — unlike \(\vec{E}\) which is radial. Its field lines are closed on themselves and obey the right-hand screw rule.

Maxwell equations for \(\vec{B}\)

$$\text{div}\,\vec{B} = 0 \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad \oiint_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S} = 0$$
Maxwell flux — local and integral forms (\(\vec{B}\) has no monopole sources)
$$\text{rot}\,\vec{B} = \mu_0\,\vec{j} \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad \oint_\mathcal{C} \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0\, I_\text{enlacé}$$
Maxwell-Ampère (static) — local and integral forms (théorème d'Ampère)

§4 — Scalar and Vector Potentials

Scalar potential \(V\) — electrostatics

In the static limit, \(\text{rot}\,\vec{E} = \vec{0}\) (Maxwell-Faraday with \(\partial\vec{B}/\partial t = 0\)). Since \(\text{rot}(\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,f) \equiv \vec{0}\) for any scalar \(f\), there exists a scalar field \(V(M)\) such that:

$$\vec{E}(M) = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V(M) \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad dV = -\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r}$$
electric field from scalar potential — static limit only

\(\vec{E}\) points in the direction of decreasing \(V\). The circulation of \(\vec{E}\) between two points \(A\) and \(B\) — along any path, since \(\vec{E}\) is conservative — gives:

$$U_{AB} = V_A - V_B = \int_A^B \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r}$$
voltage as circulation of \(\vec{E}\) — integral form

For a point charge \(q\) at \(P\), with \(V \to 0\) at infinity:

$$V(M) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\, PM} + \text{cte} \qquad \xrightarrow{\text{3D dist.}} \qquad V(M) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\iiint_\tau \frac{\rho(P)}{PM}\,d\tau + \text{cte}$$
scalar potential — point charge and volume distribution

Combining \(\text{div}\,\vec{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\) with \(\vec{E} = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V\) gives the Poisson equation:

$$\Delta V + \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} = 0 \qquad \xrightarrow{\rho=0} \qquad \Delta V = 0\;\text{(Laplace)}$$
Poisson equation — relates potential to its sources

Vector potential \(\vec{A}\) — magnetostatics and beyond

Since \(\text{div}\,\vec{B} = 0\) always (no magnetic monopoles), and \(\text{div}(\text{rot}\,\vec{A}) \equiv 0\), there always exists a vector potential \(\vec{A}(M)\) such that:

$$\vec{B} = \text{rot}\,\vec{A}$$
magnetic field from vector potential — valid always, not just in statics

\(\vec{A}\) is defined up to \(\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,f\) for any scalar \(f\) (gauge freedom). To fix this, the Coulomb gauge imposes \(\text{div}\,\vec{A} = 0\).

General potentials — dynamic regime (CM5)

In dynamic regimes (\(\partial\vec{B}/\partial t \neq 0\)), Maxwell-Faraday gives \(\text{rot}\,\vec{E} = -\partial\vec{B}/\partial t \neq \vec{0}\). Using \(\vec{B} = \text{rot}\,\vec{A}\):

$$\text{rot}\!\left(\vec{E} + \frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}\right) = \vec{0}$$
from Maxwell-Faraday in dynamic regime

Since this curl vanishes, there exists a scalar \(V\) such that the full general relation is:

$$\boxed{\vec{E} = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V - \frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}}$$
general relation \(\vec{E}\) ↔ potentials — valid always (CM5)
\(\vec{E} = -\overrightarrow{\text{grad}}\,V\) is only valid when \(\partial\vec{A}/\partial t = 0\), i.e. in the static or quasi-static limit. \(\vec{B} = \text{rot}\,\vec{A}\) has no such restriction. This asymmetry matters: in induction (CM5), a time-varying \(\vec{B}\) creates an \(\vec{E}\) that does not derive from \(V\) alone — which is why KVL breaks in the presence of a varying flux. See L1.

§5 — Voltage and the Masse Reference

\(V\) is defined up to an additive constant — a gauge choice. The voltage \(U_{AB}\) is gauge-invariant because the constant cancels:

$$U_{AB} = V_A - V_B = \int_A^B \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r}$$
voltage — gauge-invariant, path-independent (in statics)

In a circuit, we fix the gauge by choosing one node as masse (\(V = 0\) there). All other potentials are then unambiguous. In the lab: the masse is conventionally the black terminal of the generator. The oscilloscope ground clip must connect there.

§6 — Current Density and Current

The current density \(\vec{j}(M,t)\) is the charge flux per unit area per unit time at \(M\). The current \(I\) through a surface \(S\) is its flux:

$$I = \iint_S \vec{j}\cdot d\vec{S}$$
current as flux of \(\vec{j}\) — integral form

For a wire with uniform \(\vec{j}\) across section \(A\): \(I = jA\). Since \(I = dq/dt\):

$$I = \frac{dq}{dt}$$
current as rate of charge flow — circuit form

Local Ohm's law

In an isotropic conductor, drift of carriers is proportional to the driving field:

$$\vec{j} = \sigma\,\vec{E}$$
Ohm's law — local (differential) form

This is a material law, not a Maxwell equation. The macroscopic \(U = RI\) is derived from this in L2. Conventional current flows in the direction positive charges move (opposite to electron drift).

§7 — Power

The local power density (energy transferred per unit volume per unit time) is:

$$p = \vec{j}\cdot\vec{E}$$
power density — local form (W·m⁻³)

Integrating over a branch volume collapses to the circuit form for a two-terminal element with current \(I\) and voltage \(U\):

$$P = U \cdot I \qquad \text{instantaneous: } p(t) = u(t)\,i(t)$$
power — circuit form

Sign convention (poly): \(P > 0\) — element absorbs power (récepteur); \(P < 0\) — element delivers power (générateur). In AC: \(P = U_\text{eff}\,I_\text{eff}\).
