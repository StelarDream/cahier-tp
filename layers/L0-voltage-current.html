Voltage & Current

What charge, potential, and current actually are — from the Coulomb field to \(U = V_A - V_B\).

§1 — Electric Charge

Charge is the primitive. It is a property of matter, not derived from anything more fundamental at this level. Two flavours exist — positive and negative — and the net charge of an isolated system is conserved at all times.

For a continuous distribution, charge is described by the charge density \(\rho(\mathbf{r}, t)\), a scalar field giving charge per unit volume at each point in space and time. The total charge in a volume \(\mathcal{V}\) is:

$$Q = \iiint_{\mathcal{V}} \rho(\mathbf{r},t)\, dV$$
total charge in a volume

Conservation — the continuity equation

Charge cannot be created or destroyed. The local statement of this fact is the continuity equation, which links the rate of change of charge density at a point to the divergence of the current density flowing away from it:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0$$
continuity equation — differential form

Reading this: if \(\rho\) decreases at a point (\(\partial\rho/\partial t < 0\)), charge must be flowing away (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} > 0\)). Nothing is created or destroyed, only moved. This equation is the deep root of Kirchhoff's current law — we will return to it in L1.

§2 — The Electric Field

A charge distribution warps the space around it. We describe this warping by the electric field \(\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t)\): a vector field assigning to each point in space the force per unit charge that a small positive test charge would feel there.

$$\mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{E}$$
force on a charge \(q\) in field \(\mathbf{E}\)

The field is sourced by charge. Gauss's law states this precisely — in differential form:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$
Gauss's law — differential form (Maxwell I)

The divergence \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}\) at a point measures how much field is "spraying outward" from that point. Gauss says this is proportional to the charge density there. Where there is no charge, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\) — field lines neither start nor end.

The equivalent integral form, over a closed surface \(\mathcal{S}\) enclosing charge \(Q_\text{enc}\):

$$\oiint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{Q_\text{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
Gauss's law — integral form

Here \(d\mathbf{S} = \hat{n}\,dA\) is the outward-pointing area element (a vector). The left side counts how much field passes outward through the surface. The right side says this equals the enclosed charge divided by \(\varepsilon_0\), the permittivity of free space.

§3 — Electric Potential

In electrostatics — when nothing is changing in time — Faraday's law (Maxwell III) reduces to:

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0$$
electrostatic condition (static limit of Faraday's law)

A vector field with zero curl is called conservative. A theorem from vector calculus says: any conservative field can be written as the gradient of a scalar field. For \(\mathbf{E}\) we write:

$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$$
electric field from potential — differential form

The minus sign is a convention: \(\mathbf{E}\) points in the direction of decreasing \(V\), just as water flows downhill. The potential \(V(\mathbf{r})\) is a scalar field — one number per point in space, much simpler to work with than the vector field \(\mathbf{E}\).

The integral form: the potential at point \(A\), measured relative to a reference point \(\mathcal{O}\) where \(V = 0\) by convention, is the work done per unit charge bringing a test charge from \(\mathcal{O}\) to \(A\) against the field:

$$V(A) = -\int_{\mathcal{O}}^{A} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}$$
potential as a line integral — integral form

Because \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0\), this integral is path-independent: it does not matter which route you take from \(\mathcal{O}\) to \(A\), you always get the same \(V(A)\). This is what makes potential well-defined.

\(V\) is only defined up to a constant — adding any constant to \(V\) everywhere leaves \(\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\) unchanged. In circuits, we fix this freedom by choosing a reference point (the masse) and declaring \(V = 0\) there.

§4 — Voltage

\(V\) alone is ambiguous because it depends on the choice of reference. The voltage \(U_{AB}\) between two points \(A\) and \(B\) is not ambiguous — it is the difference of potentials:

$$U_{AB} = V_A - V_B$$
voltage as potential difference

In integral form, this is the line integral of \(\mathbf{E}\) along any path from \(B\) to \(A\):

$$U_{AB} = V_A - V_B = -\int_{B}^{A} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell} = \int_{A}^{B} \mathbf{E} \cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}$$
voltage as a line integral — integral form

This is what a voltmeter measures: it reports the work done per unit charge moving between its two probes, regardless of the path taken internally. Note the sign convention in the poly: \(U = V_\text{final} - V_\text{initial}\), with the arrow pointing from low to high potential.

The mass reference

In a circuit we pick one node, call it the masse, and set \(V = 0\) there. Every other potential in the circuit is then measured relative to this node. The choice is arbitrary — it does not affect any voltage \(U_{AB}\) since the reference cancels in the difference. In the lab it is conventionally the black terminal of the generator.

§5 — Current Density and Current

Charges in a conductor are free to move. When an electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) is present, they drift, and this drift constitutes a current. The fundamental quantity is the current density \(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r},t)\): a vector field giving the charge flux per unit area per unit time at each point.

The current \(I\) through a surface \(\mathcal{S}\) is the total charge crossing that surface per unit time:

$$I = \iint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$
current as flux of \(\mathbf{J}\) — integral form

For a wire where \(\mathbf{J}\) is uniform across a cross-section of area \(A\), this reduces to \(I = JA\). And since the total charge passing the cross-section is \(q(t)\), we have the time-domain form:

$$I = \frac{dq}{dt}$$
current as rate of charge flow

Ohm's law — local form

In a conductor, the drift of charge carriers is proportional to the driving field. The local (differential) form of Ohm's law is:

$$\mathbf{J} = \sigma\, \mathbf{E}$$
Ohm's law — differential form

where \(\sigma\) is the conductivity of the material (scalar for isotropic media). This is a material property, not a fundamental law — it holds statistically for metals at normal temperatures. The macroscopic circuit law \(U = RI\) is derived from this in L2.

The direction of \(\mathbf{J}\) is the direction positive charges move (or equivalently, opposite to electron drift). This is the origin of the conventional current direction convention.

§6 — Power

The electric field does work on moving charges. The power density — energy transferred per unit volume per unit time — at a point is:

$$p = \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E}$$
power density — differential form (W·m⁻³)

Integrating over the volume of a circuit branch gives the total power delivered to or extracted from that branch. For a two-terminal element carrying current \(I\) under voltage \(U\), this integral collapses to:

$$P = U \cdot I$$
power — circuit form

Sign convention follows the generator/receiver choice from the poly: \(P > 0\) means the element absorbs power (receiver); \(P < 0\) means it delivers power (generator). In instantaneous form: \(p(t) = u(t)\,i(t)\).
